NTRODUCTION As 5G networks continue to expand, the International Mobile
Telecommunications (IMT-2020) requirements define ambitious targets for a wide
range of application scenarios, with increasing influence extending also into
the Internet of Things (IoT) domain [1]. While enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB)
services have been successfully implemented in 3GPP 5G New Radio (NR) systems,
and massive machine-type communication (mMTC) is being addressed through
cellular IoT solutions like LTE-M or NB-IoT. ultra-reliable low-latency
communication (URLLC) IoT services still remains a significant challenge [2].
DECT-2020 NR has emerged as a standardized 5G technology specifically designed
for mMTC and URLLC applications, operating in the license-exempt 1.9 GHz band
utilized by legacy Digital Enhanced Cordless Telecommunications (DECT) phones.
This DECT-2020 NR standard, also referred to as DECT NR+, is announced as the
first noncellular 5G standard [3]. DECT NR+ positions itself in the IoT
landscape in a gap between traditional Wireless Personal Area Network (WPAN),
Wi-Fi and Low Power Wide Area Network (LPWAN). DECT NR+ offers significantly
higher data rates and extended range compared to typical low-rate WPAN such as
Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) or Zigbee. Its datarate is lower than Wi-Fi or
cellular services such as 5G NR. For certain parameter configurations, DECT NR+
can achieve considerable coverage and also compete with LPWAN standards such as
IEEE 802.15.4g. While recent studies primarily concentrate on theoretical
investigations concerning device density and latency [4], [2], the practical
performance aspects of DECT NR+ remain barely explored in real-world deployments
[5]. Particularly, the strategic selection of transmission parameters represents
an open research area that significantly impacts overall network performance. In
this work, we propose the first approach to link adaptation in DECT NR+. Link
adaptation defines the process of dynamically optimizing transmission parameters
based on instantaneous channel conditions and specific user requirements.
Indeed, DECT NR+ explicitly incorporates this capability through dedicated
fields in its Physical Layer Control Field, including Channel Quality Indicator
(CQI) reporting mechanisms that facilitate informed transmission parameter
adjustments. We focus on three critical performance metrics in DECT NR+: energy
consumption, data rate, and Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR). Energy consumption
remains crucial for battery-powered devices; data rate determines the system’s
capacity to handle information flow; PDR directly impacts the reliability,
latency and number of retransmissions. By characterizing the three-dimensional
trade-off space formed by these metrics, we provide an approach for link
adaptation that can dynamically adjust to meet diverse requirements. Our
methodology is based on the concept of Pareto optimality, and combines
theoretical analysis with experimental evaluation using commercial DECT NR+
hardware (Nordic Semiconductor’s nRF91 series) in indoor and outdoor
deployments. We characterize the achievable performance region and identify the
Pareto-optimal configurations. This Pareto front comprises a set of optimal
configurations where no single objective can be improved without simultaneously
degrading at least one other. Conversely, any point not on the Pareto front is
considered “dominated”: there exists at least one Paretooptimal configuration
that is superior in at least one objective and not inferior in any other. Based
on experiments in our two testbeds, we demonstrate how systems can navigate the
resulting frontier to achieve superior performance compared to a fixed parameter
selection. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II
provides an overview of the DECT NR+ physical layer. Section III describes the
link adaptation mechanism in DECT NR+. Section IV details our experimental

UNDAMENTALS DECT-2020 NR is specified in the ETSI TS 103 636 standard [6]. This
section offers a concise overview based on Release 2 dated October 2024,
covering key aspects of the physical layer, including modulation, frequency
allocations, and packet structure. A. Modulation The DECT NR+ physical layer
employs Cyclic Prefix (CP) Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM) as
its core modulation scheme. Three key modulation parameters determine the
required bandwidth and data rate. The subcarrier spacing factor µ ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8}
defines the spacing between adjacent subcarriers. Higher values of µ shorten the
OFDM symbol duration, enabling lower latency at the cost of less multipath
protection due to a shorter CP. The Fourier transform scaling factor β ∈ {1, 2,
4, 8, 12, 16} determines the number of subcarriers used. Larger β values
increase the channel bandwidth and potential data throughput, but also require
more complex Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) operations, raising computational
demands on the transceiver. The Modulation and Coding Scheme (MCS) defines both
the modulation format and the Forward Error Correction (FEC) code rate. Lower-
order MCSs use simpler modulation schemes, such as Binary Phase-Shift Keying
(BPSK) or Quadrature Phase-Shift Keying (QPSK) and lower code rates for
robustness, while higher-order MCSs are based on Quadrature Amplitude Modulation
(QAM) and offer increased payload capacity at the expense of error resilience.

III. L INK A DAPTATION In DECT NR+, link adaptation, i.e. the adjustment of
transmit power MCS and PT X , is based on a dual-channel transmission structure
consisting of the PCC and PDC. The PCC always uses a fixed MCS, ensuring it can
always be decoded by the receiver, while the PDC’s MCS is dynamically selected
by the transmitter. Both channels are transmitted with the same power per
transmission, but PT X itself can vary between transmissions to meet the
required quality with minimal energy. The PCC includes the Physical Layer
Control Field, which is shown in Fig. 2. It informs the receiver about the
transmission parameters used for the PDC, including the selected MCS and
transmit power. This allows the receiver to estimate the current path loss.
Additionally, the Physical Layer Control Field contains Feedback Info, where the
receiver can report a CQI, indicating the highest MCS it can decode under
current conditions. The transmitter uses this CQI, along with calculated PDR and
service quality targets, to adapt the MCS. The specific adaptation algorithm is
not standardized and is left to vendor implementation, allowing differentiation
and optimization. This design also supports interoperability across A. Hardware

There is no analytical expression for the objective function, as it depends on
the current channel conditions. Therefore, an iterative approach to channel
quality estimation is essential. The system must continuously refine its
understanding of channel conditions through both active traffic measurements and
dedicated pilot sequences. The estimation process involves multiple iterations
where transmissions are made at various power levels and MCS configurations.
Initially, measurements show significant variance, but as iterations increase,
the PDR curves stabilize. This convergence typically requires 5 to 10 iterations
with 100 packets per (PT X , MCS) configuration. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the
PDR versus transmit power curves exhibit distinctly different characteristics
between different environments. The indoor scenario, characterized by non-LOS
conditions, produces a steep “waterfall” shape with a sharp transition between
low and high PDR regions. This abrupt transition occurs due to multipath
effects, signal reflections, and obstructions typical in indoor settings. In
contrast, the outdoor testbed curves display a more gradual slope because of LOS
conditions where primarily free space path loss affects signal propagation,
resulting in a more gradual degradation of signal quality with distance. Not
only is the shape of the curves different among the testbeds, the gap between
distinct MCS levels also is. This paper focuses on on static networks, it is
therefore important to acknowledge that channel conditions inevitably change
over time. Iterative recreation of the PDR curves is crucial for channel quality
estimation, and serves as the fundamental building block of parameter
optimization frameworks. VI. A PARETO O PTIMALITY A PPROACH A. Algorithm For
this multi-objective optimization problem, we apply the principle of Pareto
optimality [7]. This widely recognized concept has also found significant
application in areas such as multi-user communications engineering [8]. We
define the parameter space P as the set of all valid transmission
configurations: p = (PTX , L, MCS) ∈ P Each configuration p yields a performance
vector: u(p) = (R(p), −ETX (p), PDR(p)) ∈ R3 The minus sign in the energy
dimension ensures that all objectives are formulated as maximization problems.
We define the multi-objective utility region as the union over all feasible
configurations: K [ U≜ {uk } ⊆ R3 k=1 where K is the total number of valid
combinations in P. A point u ∈ U is Pareto optimal if there exists no other
point v ∈ U such that: v ≥ u and v ̸= u where the inequality is component-wise.
In other words, no other configuration achieves better or equal performance in
all objectives and strictly better in at least one. A point is called “weak”
Pareto optimal if no other point is strictly better in all objectives: ̸ ∃v ∈ U
such that v > u The set of all Pareto optimal points forms the Pareto frontier
of U, which represents the boundary of efficient trade-offs between the
objectives. To compute the Pareto frontier in practice, we proceed as follows:
1) Enumerate all valid configurations pk from the parameter space P. 2) For each
configuration, compute the corresponding utility vector uk . 3) Compare all
utility vectors pairwise to identify nondominated points: • A point uk is
dominated if there exists another point uj such that uj ≥ uk and uj ̸= uk . •
All non-dominated points form the Pareto frontier. This brute-force approach is
feasible due to the discrete nature of the DECT NR+ parameter space. For larger
spaces, more efficient algorithms such as Pareto sorting or evolutionary multi-
objective optimization are usually employed. B. Practical Evaluation Fig. 7
presents a visualization of the three-dimensional trade-off space for the
outdoor LOS testbed. The plot shows the performance vector u for all possible
operating points p in the parameter space P. The white triangle points represent
the Pareto front, consisting of those configurations for which improving one
objective necessarily degrades another. These Pareto-optimal points offer the
most efficient operating conditions for different application requirements. Any
point not on the Pareto front is “dominated”: there exists at least one
configuration on the front that is superior in at least one objective and no
worse in any other. The color scheme in Fig. 7 corresponds to the one in Fig. 6,
and reveals how operating points corresponding to different MCS appear to be
“stacked” along the data rate axis. For PDR values below 80%, the Pareto front
is mostly formed by operating points using MCS 4. This is due to the higher
number of bytes that can be transmitted per slot: shorter packet lengths
compared to lower-order MCS can be used, resulting in a smaller energy per
transmission. However, for a target PDR greater than 80%, lower MCS form the
Pareto front. Also visible in Fig. 6, the performance in terms of PDR is very
similar in the domain greater than -10 dBm for MCS 23 and MCS 0-1, respectively.
This means that, due to the higher transport block size for the packet slot
length, it is more efficient to choose MCS 1 instead of MCS 0 for instance.

Title: Waßmann et al. - 2024 - Performance of DECT-2020 NR in an industrial environment for varying RF bands

Summary:

This paper addresses the central research challenge of the selected DECT-2020 NR / 5G / wireless networking domain. It focuses on how the proposed design or algorithm improves reliability, coverage, resource management, or performance for emerging URLLC, mMTC, or mesh-networked applications.

Problem and motivation:
—As there are no publications regarding the performance of DECT-2020 NR in real-
life scenarios yet, this work constitutes a first performance assessment of
DECT-2020 NR in the 450, 1350 and 3700 MHz frequency bands at different
numerologies with bandwidths ranging from 1.728 to 13.824 MHz. To show the
benefits of DECT-2020 NR, it is compared to classic DECT in a Rayleigh fading
channel. In the course of this evaluation, performance metrics such as Bit Error
Rate (BER) and Packet Error Rate (PER) were considered. Furthermore, MATLAB-
generated DECT-2020 NR packets were transmitted utilizing GNU Radio and Ettus
USRPs. The measurements were conducted in an industrial production line.
Overall, the packet error rates were found to be the best at 450 MHz, with a
packet loss of less than 5 %. At 1350 MHz, a packet error rate of 15 % was
observed, whereas at 3700 MHz, a maximum value of 10 % was recorded. The
differences in PER for the different frequencies are attributed to the internal
losses at higher frequencies in the used USRP B210s. Another aspect of this work
focuses on characterizing the indoor channel of the production line using OFDM-
based channel sounding. On average, an RMS delay spread of 65 ns was measured
for a bandwidth of 13.824 MHz in the selected frequency bands.
