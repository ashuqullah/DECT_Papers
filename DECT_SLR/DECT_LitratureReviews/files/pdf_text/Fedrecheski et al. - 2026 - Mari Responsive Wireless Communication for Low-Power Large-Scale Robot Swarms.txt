                     1. Introduction

                        Large-scale robotic swarms are the next frontier in distributed autonomous




                                                               r
                     systems. Swarms of micro-robots [1] could perform inspection and damage
                     repair in hard-to-reach places, such as engines, building interiors, and satellites

                                                            er

                     user interfaces [3] and minimally invasive medicine [4]. Large swarms of small
                                                 pe
                     robots can also be useful to experiment with swarm behavior in more realistic
                     scenarios before committing to full-scale robots, helping to close the simulation-

                                      ot

                          tn

                rin

                     lecting logs.

                     2. Related Work

                        We review communication technologies used in swarm robotics, including
                                      ot


                     solutions to tackle scalability, low-latency, and mobility.
                        Wi-Fi and BLE are prevalent in the field of swarm robotics due to widespread
                          tn



                     support. Wi-Fi offers low latency and high throughput, enabling real-time trans-
                     mission of rich information such as video streams and environment mapping.
                     Limitations of Wi-Fi for swarms of micro-robots include relatively high power
                     consumption, in the order of 200 mA (comparable to a motor), and scalability,
                rin




                     due to its contention-based medium access. Some of the largest Wi-Fi -based
                     swarms in the literature achieve 22 [19], 50 [15], and 63 [16] nodes each. A
                     solution with 50 robots [15] reported packet loss of 40-50% due to high node
     ep




                     density.
                        BLE provides a low-power alternative, typically drawing around 10 mA dur-
                     ing transmission, an order of magnitude less than typical robot motors. Al-
Pr




                                                              5




This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=6571600
                                                                                           ed
                     though its maximum throughput of 2 Mbps is not enough for transferring rich
                     media in real time, it is sufficient for robot control. In terms of scalability, the




                                                                                        iew
                     BLE software stack typically achieves 5 [3, 10] to 10 [11] nodes connected to a
                     single gateway. In the HERMITS swarm [3], connecting 70 robots required a
                     total of 14 BLE gateways. Bluetooth mesh enables larger coverage areas [20],
                     but its flooding-based mechanism makes it unsuitable for real-time control.




                                                                             ev
                        An alternative consists in leveraging 2.4 GHz radios, sometimes reusing BLE
                     modulation, and implementing a tailored link layer for the needs of multi-robot
                     control. This is the case of the Zooids robot swarm [8, 21], which achieved




                                                                r
                     32 robots with 3 radio gateways. Similarly, the CrazySwarm system [14] mod-
                     ified the communication layer of the CrazyFlie quadrotor platform to tolerate


                                                             er
                     packet loss and support compression, enabling control for a swarm of 49 quadro-
                     tors. It used 3 gateways, built out of a 2.4 GHz radio on the nRF24LU1+ MCU.
                        To allow scaling up to 1024 robots, the Kilobot [22] uses infrared commu-
                                                 pe
                     nication, allowing massive OTAP of all robots. Due to bandwidth constraints,
                     this setup does not allow real-time control.
                        The IEEE 802.15.4 standard [23] offers a PHY that is appropriate for low-
                     power devices such as wireless sensors and Internet of Things (IoT) devices. It
                                      ot

                     is used by higher-layer protocols such as ZigBee [23] and Thread [24], which use
                     static mesh topologies. The IEEE 802.15.4e [17] amendment introduces TSCH
                     support to IEEE 802.15.4, improving its scalability, power consumption, and re-
                          tn



                     silience to interference. It has been studied and deployed in both academic [25]
                     and industrial [26] settings, demonstrating the effectiveness of the TSCH ap-
                     proach. The IETF 6TiSCH stack further standardizes the integration of TSCH
                rin




                     in IP networks [18], but mainly targets static nodes. The maximum speed of
                     250 kbps found in IEEE 802.15.4 networks can be a limiting factor for real-time
                     control at very large scale, i.e. thousands of micro-robots.
                        TBLE [27] implements TSCH mesh routing on top of BLE, achieving 20%
     ep




                     lower latency when compared to IEEE 802.15.4e networks. Due to faster data
                     rates of up to 2 Mbps, it achieves a slot duration of 4 ms, shorter than the 10 ms
                     slots in IEEE 802.15.4e. TBLE does not address the challenge of mobility in

                        A common solution to improve scalability in wireless systems consists in




                                                                                        iew
                     adding more gateways. This can also help to increase the coverage area, enabling
                     larger deployments. Cellular systems such as 5G are built for ultra-large scale
                     and have mobility built-in, but consume considerable power and are expensive
                     to deploy. DECT-NR+, a cheaper alternative focused on industrial sensing and




                                                                             ev
                     control, still consumes hundreds of mW [28], making it unsuitable for power-
                     constrained micro-robots. Wi-Fi supports handovers, while standard BLE does
                     not. Existing low-power swarm robotic systems primarily use multiple gateways




                                                                r
                     to tackle scalability [3, 8, 14, 12], leaving the mobility aspect less explored.
                        Standard TSCH allows for extended range via mesh network formation, but


                                                             er
                     does not support mobile nodes. One critical issue is the time needed to leave
                     one neighbor and join another, which can take several minutes [29]. Adapted
                     scheduling algorithms have been proposed to reduce re-join time, at the cost of
                                                 pe
                     increased communication delay [30]. Centralized scheduling schemes have been
                     shown to be more effective at handling mobility in TSCH systems [31].
                        Haxhibeqiri et al. propose a synchronized single-hop multi-gateway (SHMG)
                     architecture using TSCH to connect a set of mobile robots [2]. The solution uses
                                      ot

                     a dedicated Precise Time Protocol (PTP) server to keep gateways synchronized.
                     This allows robots to roam around an area covered by multiple gateways. Since
                     the gateways are synchronized to a precision of 1 ms, robots communicate with a
                          tn



                     single logical gateway, when in reality more than one gateway may be receiving
                     the same message. A backend server provides message de-duplication. One lim-
                     itation of the SHMG architecture is communication overhead, as the schedule is
                rin




                     replicated for each gateway. To solve this, a Location-Aware Scheduling Algo-
                     rithm with Rescheduling (LASA-R) has been proposed to build and maintain an
                     efficient TSCH schedule for robots considering their location [32]. The SHMG
                     architecture has been validated with up to 4 robots and 2 gateways. Although
     ep




                     it is expected to scale to larger deployments, a fundamental limitation is the
                     fact that all gateways operate as a single logical gateway, hindering horizontal
                     scalability.

                        A Shared-Downstream Dedicated-Upstream (SD-DU) scheduling scheme has
                     been proposed to improve the efficiency of the SHMG architecture [33]. In SD-




                                                                                      iew
                     DU, downstream traffic can be shared across a set of robots, while upstream
                     traffic is dedicated to each robot. We adopt a similar approach in Mari.
                        The SHMG architecture differs from Mari since our system does not require
                     gateway synchronization, increasing scalability as each gateway serves a different




                                                                           ev
                     set of robots. Moreover, since robots themselves control the handover procedure,
                     network infrastructure remains minimal and easy to deploy. Finally, Mari uses
                     a semi-static slot allocation scheme, with minimal communication overhead for




                                                               r
                     scheduling.
                        Ben Yaala et al. [34] show that unsynchronized co-located TSCH networks


                                                            er
                     suffer from collision periods due to clock drift, and propose cooperative resyn-
                     chronization among border routers to mitigate the effect. Their analysis assumes
                     IEEE 802.15.4e parameters with 15 ms slots and 16 channels, where blackout
                                                 pe
                     periods can exceed 100 s. Mari operates with shorter slots (1.7 ms), a larger
                     channel pool (37 BLE data channels), and single-hop communication, reducing
                     both the duration and probability of such collisions. Results in Section 5 confirm
                     that PDR stays above 85% even when using eight co-located Mari gateways.
                                     ot

                     deployments, at the cost of considerable power consumption and high deploy-

                          tn



                     it at scale. Mari is the first system to enable responsive control of hundreds of
                     mobile robots using BLE radios. While inspired by multi-gateway solutions,
                     Mari provides a novel approach that does not require gateway synchronization.
                rin




                     Similarly, Mari tackles the challenge of mobility in TSCH systems by using cen-

                     3. Design of Mari

                            We propose the Micro-robot Access Radio Infrastructure (Mari), a wireless
                                                                                                                 er
                                                                                     pe
                     link layer and infrastructure that enables responsive communication with large
                     and dense swarms of low-power robots.

                     3.1. Requirements

                            The requirements for the design of Mari are:
                                                          ot


                            • Scalability (R1): future robot swarms will comprise hundreds to thou-
                                 sands of units, so Mari should natively support large scale deployments.
                                tn



                            • Responsiveness (R2): researchers should be able to observe and interac-
                                 tively control the robots, as well as to tune the network parameters, e.g. to
                                 allow remote control loops. Unlike traditional Wireless Sensor Networks
                rin




                                 (WSN) applications that optimize primarily for battery life, Mari must
                                 provide low end-to-end latency, in the order of 50 to 500 ms.

                            • Throughput for OTAP (R3): reprogramming large scale swarms can be
     ep




                                 extremely time consuming if done manually, hindering rapid experimenta-
                                 tion. Mari should provide sufficient throughput to support OTAP update
                                 of a full swarm in a matter of minutes.
Pr




                                                                                                                 9




This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=6571600
                                                                                        ed
                        • Fast association (R4): when a robot turns on or resets, or when a gateway
                           suddenly becomes available, the whole swarm should rapidly associate,




                                                                                     iew
                           ideally in a few seconds.

                        • Seamless handovers (R5): handover events must be fast to minimize im-
                           pacts on responsiveness. From a single robot perspective, the delay should
                           be comparable to the average latency.




                                                                            ev
                        • Low-power radio (R6): the radio should consume a negligible amount of
                           power when compared to the robot motors. Given that motors in small
                           robots typically draw around 300 mA, and assuming a supercapacitor-




                                                               r
                           based, low energy density design [35], a continuous radio consumption of


                                                            er
                           10 mA should allow operation for at least 2 h.

                        A final, non-functional requirement is that the system should be practical to
                     deploy (R7), to facilitate adoption by the research community. That means it
                                                  pe
                     should be low-cost to allow scaling, ideally relying solely on COTS components.
                     Infrastructure complexity should also be kept low, as to minimize deployment
                     overhead.

                     BLE as the PHY layer due to its low-power operation (R6), data rate of up to
                     2 Mbps (R2, R3), and low-cost availability as a COTS item (R7). Mari sup-
                     ports multiple non-coordinated gateways, enabling horizontal scalability (R1).
                     To enable node mobility, we incorporate a fast node-controlled handover logic
     ep




                     (R5). We provide mari-fw 1 , the link layer implementation in C for both node

                     3.5. Scheduling
                                       ot

                        Mari defines four types of slots: Beacon (B), Shared uplink (S), Downlink
                     (D), and Uplink (U). Beacon slots are used to advertise the network: gateways
                     emit Beacon frames, nodes listen for them. To speed up network discovery,
                          tn



                     Beacon slots hop through a limited set of frequencies, only using the 3 standard
                     BLE advertising frequencies. Other slots hop through the remaining 37 BLE
                     frequencies.
                rin




                        Shared uplink slots are used by nodes to join a gateway and are subject

                     3.8. Handover

                        As we show in Section 3.5.1, the largest schedule in Mari supports up to
                     102 nodes while having an acceptable latency. While that scale already sur-




                                                               r
                     passes most of the current literature for low-power robots, it is still not enough


                                                            er
                     to connect future swarms of thousands of robots. In particular, the DotBot
                     testbed aims to connect 1,000 robots. We therefore adopt a horizontal scalabil-
                     ity approach, where, to support more nodes, we simply add more gateways to
                                                 pe
                     the system. This implies the need for a handover mechanism.
                        To enable node mobility with fast handovers, Mari nodes scan for beacons
                     in the background. That is, while still in joined state, instead of having the
                     radio sleep during unused slots, it turns on for receiving Beacon frames from
                     other gateways. Specifically, a node will use the Uplink and Shared uplink slots
                                     ot


                     not assigned to itself for background scanning. Since motors typically consume
                     10×-100× more power than a BLE radio, continuous background listening has
                          tn



                     negligible impact on energy usage. Moreover, background scanning can be dis-
                     abled when the robot stops, since no handover is needed when it is not moving.
                        After BGScan ms receiving beacons, the node selects the gateway with best
                     RSSI, and, if the signal is significantly better, it disconnects from the current
                rin




                     gateway to join the new one. This is shown in Fig. 5, where the node changes
                     state from joined to synced. The node uses a difference in RSSI to make the
                     handover decision: ∆RSSI = RSSInew − RSSIcurrent . A handover is triggered
     ep




                     only if ∆RSSI > Hth , where Hth is a predefined hysteresis threshold. We
                     empirically found Hth = 24 dB when using an nRF5340-DK device as a gateway
                     and an nRF52840-Dongle as a node. From the old gateway’s perspective, the
Pr




                                                            19




This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=6571600
                                                                                         ed
                     timeout N odeT imeout soon triggers (typically less than one second), leading to
                     the disassociation of the node.




                                                                                      iew
                        A worst-case scenario in this approach is when dozens or hundreds of robots
                     move together, e.g. in a flocking behavior. In such cases, if the association
                     time is too high, robots may appear unresponsive. On the other hand, since
                     robots move at different positions, they are expected to observe different RSSI




                                                                           ev
                     values, and therefore perform the handover at slightly different times, mitigating
                     contention issues. We experimentally validate this assumption in Section 4.
                        The multi-gateway handover approach allows Mari to scale to several hun-




                                                               r
                     dreds, potentially thousands of nodes. Since the nodes control all the handover
                     decision, gateways do not need to coordinate about the assigned Uplink slots


                                                            er
                     in the TSCH schedule, or even with the MQTT Broker, simplifying deployment
                     (R7). When a gateway receives a message from the Broker, it simply checks
                     the destination address, and if it is not for one of its nodes, it drops the mes-
                                                 pe
                     sage. Finally, unlike synchronized multi-gateway systems [2, 32], Mari gateways
                     are entirely uncoordinated, offloading handover decision to nodes for improved
                     scalability (R1).

                     5. Integration with the DotBot Testbed




                                                                                     iew
                        This section presents preliminary results of integrating Mari with a large-
                     scale swarm robotics testbed. As this integration is still ongoing, we report it
                     separately from the main evaluation: we describe the integration process and
                     key performance metrics, noting that these experiments were conducted in a
                     less controlled environment.




                                                                           ev
                        As part of the OpenSwarm project, we built a testbed of 1,000 DotBots. The
                     testbed, shown in Fig. 1, enables wireless instrumentation, remote firmware up-
                     dates, and live remote control of low-power robots. Each robot uses an nRF5340




                                                                   r
                     MCU [38] with a BLE transceiver and a 240 F supercapacitor-based energy
                     system [35]. Under continuous operation, the supercapacitors can power the
                     low-power radio (R6) for a few hours.
                                                                er
                        We modify the DotBot testbed software to use Mari as the communication
                     layer. First, we replace the radio link layer of both the robot and the gateway
                                                 pe
                     with mari-fw. Next, we update the infrastructure to use Mari’s MQTT topics
                     and modify the user software to use marilib as the communication adapter.
                        In an experimental deployment, we use 8 Mari gateways to connect up to
                     724 DotBots, as shown in Fig. 1. The Mari gateways are configured to use the
                                      ot


                     huge schedule (max. 102 nodes), implying a maximum capacity of 816 nodes.
                        Users can flash firmware, start and stop experiments, and control robots re-
                          tn



                     motely. We successfully flash a 4 kB firmware image in 4 min. To enable remote
                     control, we integrate marilib with PyDotBot5 , an environment for interacting
                     with DotBot swarms that provides a web-based frontend for real-time monitor-
                     ing of robot state, as well as a REST/Websocket interface for remote control.
                rin




                     We develop Python scripts that interact with PyDotBot to command the entire
                     swarm. We run an epidemic color-propagation experiment: a single robot is set
                     to red, triggering its neighbors to turn red in turn, causing the color to spread
     ep




                     across the swarm in real time. The propagation of the LED color and robot

                     6. Discussion

                     Scalability. We thoroughly evaluate Mari in a setup with up to 200 nodes and
                     two gateways, and demonstrate a large scale deployment with 724 nodes using
                     eight gateways. We achieve linear increase in latency up to 100 nodes using a
                                     ot


                     single gateway, and no significant latency increase when using more gateways to
                     connect up to 724 nodes. We achieve high PDR even at the scale of hundreds of
                          tn



                     nodes, with an average PDR above 85% and an average end-to-end latency of
                     270 ms. In future work, we plan to investigate how gateway placement influences
                     cross-interference, as gateway deployments of the same cardinality have different
                     interference profiles depending on the gateway placement. The current version
                rin




                     of Mari represents a significant advance over the state-of-the-art, where Wi-Fi
                     based swarms connected 50 robots with 40 % packet loss [15] and BLE swarms
                     needed 14 gateways to connect 70 robots [3].
     ep




                     Latency. We present a configurable scheduling approach that allows average la-
                     tency between 45.7 ms and 269.3 ms for slotframes of capacity 10 and 102 nodes,
                     respectively. These results are compatible with the literature on robot swarms.
Pr




                                                            31




This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=6571600
                                                                                         ed
                     The SHMG architecture reports latency figures between 300 and 500 ms [2].
                     Other systems report communication rates of “a few Hertz” [12] and control




                                                                                      iew
                     loops running every 0.27 s [15]. While CrazySwarm [14] reports latency as low
                     as 26 ms for an average of 16.3 nodes per gateway, frame MTUs are only a
                     few bytes long and the protocol only supports broadcast. Finally, since the
                     mari-fw implementation is parameterized, it is possible to tune the MTU and




                                                                           ev
                     other configurations to achieve more aggressive latency profiles.

                     Reliability. Mari intentionally omits link-layer acknowledgments to maximize
                     throughput, leaving reliability to upper-layer protocols. This is an important de-




                                                               r
                     sign consideration: applications must implement their own mechanisms (e.g. re-
                     tries and timeouts) to handle packet loss. The DotBot testbed integration il-

                                                            er
                     lustrates this trade-off: OTAP initially failed for a portion of the 724-robot
                     deployment due to insufficient retransmission timeouts at the application layer,
                                                 pe
                     an issue resolved by tuning these parameters (Section 5). As future work, Mari
                     can be integrated with a standard network stack, where protocols such as TCP
                     could provide reliable communication.

                     Security. Mari currently does not include a security layer, which is acceptable
                                     ot

                     for experimental deployments in controlled environments, such as in robotic
                     testbeds and research experiments. As a provisional measure, basic confiden-
                     tiality and authentication can be handled at higher layers if needed. In fu-
                          tn



                     ture work, we plan to integrate secure join and frame protection mechanisms
                     in Mari. This includes the use of standardized protocols for security in low-
                     power networks, such as Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman Over COSE (EDHOC) [39]
                rin




                     for key establishment, CBOR Object Signing and Encryption (COSE) [40] for
                     message protection, and Lightweight Authorization over EDHOC (ELA) [41]
                     for automated node enrollment. Furthermore, fast security resumption during
                     handovers can be achieved via EDHOC-PSK [42].
     ep




                     Interoperability. Although Mari was developed and evaluated in a standalone
                     fashion, in the future it could be integrated with a standard networking stack.

                     The first 6 bytes of identifiers defined in the frame format (Fig. 3) can be used as
                     MAC addresses, and marilib could implement a TUN/TAP interface to expose




                                                                                        iew
                     Mari as a regular network interface as recognized by the operating system. This
                     approach would impose higher complexity at the node side, as it would also
                     have to manage more complex network stacks carried over Mari frames.

                     Scheduling. The use of semi-static scheduling brings a balance between imple-




                                                                             ev
                     mentation simplicity, handover speed, and achieving communication require-
                     ments (R1-R5). In the future, instead of specifying a fixed set of schedules,
                     a template could be used, and a protocol for dynamic schedule update could




                                                                r
                     be specified to handle association and disassociation of new nodes. Impacts on
                     relevant metrics should be considered, such as end-to-end latency, association
                     time, and handover duration.

                     7. Conclusion

                        This paper introduces Mari, a platform demonstrated to connect more than
                     700 small robots while providing responsive communication and fast firmware
                     updates. We use TSCH to enable predictable latency and mitigate interfer-
                                      ot

                     ences, and provide four semi-static schedules that balance several requirements,
                     including latency, scalability, and association time. We perform extensive eval-
                     uation on real hardware, showing how Mari can associate 100 nodes in less than
                          tn



                     4 s and achieve latency of 45.7 ms and 269.3 ms for 10 and 102 connected nodes,
                     respectively. We simulate a flocking behavior with 100 real nodes, achieving an
                     average handover time of only 0.34 s even under severe contention. We demon-
                rin




                     strate 200 nodes connected to two gateways achieving latency of 274 ms, as well
                     as an uplink and downlink PDR above 93% and 95%, respectively. Our solu-
                     tion is open-source and runs on COTS low-power micro-controllers. Besides the
                     firmware, we provide libraries that allow users to plug-and-play Mari in their
     ep




                     swarm deployments. We integrated Mari as the communication layer in the
                     DotBot testbed, where we validated real time robot control and the firmware
Pr




                                                             33

                                                                                        ed
                     update of 600 robots in less than 4 minutes. We plan to use Mari to connect
                     the full testbed comprising 1,000 robots.
